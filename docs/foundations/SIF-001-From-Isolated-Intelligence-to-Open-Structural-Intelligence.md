## Abstract

Artificial intelligence is commonly organized around individual models, algorithms, applications, or agent systems. Such systems can be powerful, but treating any one architecture as the definitive container of intelligence risks obscuring the wider landscape of computational methods, structural representations, specialized evaluators, and future discoveries. This paper introduces the **Structural Intelligence Framework (SIF)** as an open, evolving architecture for identifying, locating, composing, governing, evaluating, and growing intelligence structures. SIF is not a replacement for large language models (LLMs): it places LLMs prominently among known intelligence capabilities while treating them as participants in a larger ecosystem. We propose four non-exclusive functional domains—**Localization**, **Per-Node Intelligence**, **Universal Control and Scoring**, and **Residual-Based Structural Growth**—connected through interoperable names, types, contracts, and relay mechanisms. A dual-map architecture links problem requirements with available capabilities, while a structural-precedent registry supports reuse, adaptation, and discovery. Adversarial evaluation and counter-evidence provide candidate mechanisms for testing structural claims before deployment. Finally, we articulate the research hypothesis that sustained structural composition and validated growth may expand not only problem-solving performance but also the system's *structural perception horizon*: the distinctions, relations, and possibilities it can represent and investigate. These are architectural proposals and testable hypotheses, not claims of a fully implemented or empirically validated universal intelligence system.

**Keywords:** Structural Intelligence Framework; open-ended intelligence; intelligence localization; per-node intelligence; structural precedents; dual maps; universal contracts; relay API; scoring forest; counter-evidence; adversarial sandbox; structural growth; perception horizon.

## 1. Introduction: Beyond the Isolated Intelligence Paradigm

Much of AI development is described in terms of a model, a family of models, or a narrowly defined agent architecture. This viewpoint is useful for measuring individual capabilities, but incomplete as a description of the total space of intelligence-bearing computation. A language model, a rule engine, a constraint solver, a simulator, a time-series forecaster, a graph-search procedure, and a human-guided policy system each have different strengths, limitations, assumptions, and evidence requirements.

The central question of SIF is therefore not *Which single model will contain all intelligence?* It is:

> **How can known and future intelligence structures be discovered, organized, matched to problems, composed into larger systems, evaluated under explicit criteria, and revised as evidence accumulates?**

This question shifts attention from isolated model capability to **structural organization of capabilities**. It does not imply that larger assemblies always outperform specialized systems: integration has costs, and a simple method may be superior when the task is narrow or the operational constraints are strict.

SIF begins with three commitments:

1. **Openness:** no present inventory is presumed complete; new structures and perspectives may be added, while existing ones may be revised or retired.
2. **Pluralism:** heterogeneous intelligence methods can coexist without being forced into one internal algorithm or representation.
3. **Evidence-governed evolution:** claims of improvement require explicit conditions, counter-evidence, and evaluation—not merely additional complexity.

## 2. What Counts as an Intelligence Structure?

Within SIF, an **intelligence structure** is a computational or organizational capability that can contribute to solving, evaluating, interpreting, or reformulating an intelligence problem under an identifiable context. It may be a model, algorithm, rule system, graph, evaluator, workflow, simulation, reusable principle, or composite system.

This definition is deliberately functional. Not every numerical program independently exhibits learning or autonomous reasoning; rather, ordinary computation may become a useful **per-node capability** when placed inside an intelligence problem-solving workflow.

An intelligence structure can be described along multiple perspectives:

- **Function:** localization, inference, optimization, scoring, governance, growth, or a combination.
- **Representation:** natural language, metric vectors, graphs, constraints, symbolic rules, state spaces, or hybrid forms.
- **Scope:** atomic operation, node, composite subsystem, or higher-order assembly.
- **Interface:** required inputs, outputs, contexts, contracts, and relay mechanisms.
- **Evidence:** known successes, failures, uncertainty, operating costs, and applicable conditions.
- **Authority:** whether it may propose, recommend, simulate, or execute an action.

A structure can occupy multiple roles. For example, an LLM may route tasks, reason within a node, generate candidate hypotheses, or critique another model. SIF therefore favors **multi-perspective classification** over a rigid taxonomy that assigns every structure to exactly one category.

## 3. Four Functional Domains of SIF

The proposed SIF backbone consists of four **functional domains**, not four mutually exclusive software layers.

### 3.1 Localization of Intelligence Problems

Localization identifies the relevant context, structural region, knowledge, capability, or execution destination for a problem. It extends beyond information retrieval to **intelligence addressing**: locating *where and how intelligence should be applied*.

Candidate mechanisms include:

1. **LLMs and foundation models**, prominent due to their broad semantic and contextual utility.
2. **Metric-space CCC representations**, organizing shared structure and meaningful residual differences.
3. **Metric Differential Trees (MDT)** for structural localization and progressive refinement.
4. **Two-phase search** for partial targets and staged candidate verification.
5. **Intelligence relay networks** for cross-node and cross-system routing.
6. **CallingGraphs** for dependency-aware task and action localization.
7. **Core + Delta structures** for reusing common patterns while preserving differences.
8. **Universal naming and typing** for consistent identity and discovery.
9. **World models and state estimation**, which provide situational information but also operate across other domains.

Localization should report uncertainty and alternatives where feasible. A confidently selected but wrong node can undermine the entire downstream workflow.

### 3.2 Per-Node Intelligence

Per-node intelligence is the open ecosystem of methods available at a selected location. LLMs occupy a prominent first position, but are not the only admissible capability. Other families include rules, two-way CCC and principle engines, event triggers, graph algorithms, numerical linear algebra, nearest-neighbor retrieval, mathematical programming, optimization, simulation, time-series models, planning, and hybrid compositions.

The aim is not to rank these families on one universal scale. Each capability must be assessed against its task, constraints, costs, evidence, and failure modes. A small deterministic checker may be more appropriate than a generative model for a safety-critical invariant.

### 3.3 Universal Control Planes and Scoring Structures

Control and scoring address different questions from capability generation:

- **Policy:** what is permitted, forbidden, delegated, or subject to approval?
- **Scoring:** which candidates best satisfy declared objectives and constraints?
- **Evidence and audit:** what supports the evaluation, and what remains uncertain?

Scoring trees, forests, folding/unfolding, and perspective-specific evaluators can organize deep decision processes. However, **evaluation does not imply authorization**. A high-scoring action is not automatically an action the system may execute.

This distinction becomes more important as structural assemblies acquire greater operational reach.

### 3.4 Residual-Based Structural Growth

Residual-based growth begins when an existing representation, localization path, evaluator, relay, or capability fails to account for important observations or requirements. Residuals may include prediction errors, missing perspectives, structural mismatches, governance gaps, or incompatibilities between nodes.

Not every residual merits a new structure: it may reflect noise, data defects, resource constraints, or a transient anomaly. Candidate growth should follow classification, comparison with existing precedents, sandbox testing, and explicit acceptance criteria.

Growth can add or revise localization methods, per-node capabilities, relay pathways, evaluation mechanisms, and higher-order composites. It can also **remove** structures shown to be ineffective or unsafe.

## 4. Two Complementary Maps: Problems and Capabilities

A usable SIF needs more than a catalog of available algorithms. We propose two linked maps:

**Structural Capability Map:** what capabilities exist, under which contracts, assumptions, and evidence?  
**Structural Problem-Solving Map:** what does a problem require, how can it be decomposed, and which structures could satisfy its requirements?

This recalls the distinction between a **Task CallingGraph** and an **Action CallingGraph**. The analogy is not an identity: SIF applies it across heterogeneous intelligence structures, not only software calls.

A bidirectional matching mechanism supports:

- **Demand → Capability:** retrieve and adapt structures for a known problem.
- **Capability → Demand:** discover new uses or problem formulations enabled by an emerging capability.
- **Gap identification:** identify needs that no available capability satisfies adequately.

Matching may yield direct reuse, composition, adaptation, or a new growth hypothesis. Each result should preserve the reasons for the match and its known limits.

## 5. Structural Precedents: Learning from Existing Solutions

SIF treats reusable intelligence structures partly like **legal precedents**: a previous solution is informative because of its relevant facts, assumptions, reasoning, and boundaries—not because it must be copied blindly.

A **Structural Precedent Registry** should retain, at minimum:

- Identity, version, and provenance.
- Problem signatures and applicable contexts.
- Capability descriptions and interface contracts.
- Required assumptions and resource constraints.
- Evidence of performance and known failure cases.
- Counter-evidence, restrictions, and revision history.
- Relations to alternative or composable structures.

The registry must include **failed and limited precedents**, not only successful examples. Otherwise it risks becoming a repository of unchallenged success claims. A precedent can be superseded, narrowed, or withdrawn when new evidence warrants it.

## 6. Interoperability Without Architectural Uniformity

An open intelligence framework cannot require every participant to share one internal model. It instead needs interoperable descriptions and controlled exchanges.

We propose a cross-cutting foundation combining:

- **Universal Name:** stable identity, discovery, provenance, and version reference.
- **Universal Type:** extensible descriptions of semantics, capabilities, and constraints.
- **Relay API:** invocation, handoff, status, results, and optional delegation.
- **Structural Intelligence Contract (SIC):** the declared input/output signatures, contexts, evidence, policies, and failure conditions governing interaction.

A minimal relay may expose operations conceptually equivalent to `describe`, `match`, `invoke`, `status`, `result`, and `cancel`; extended operations can include delegation, counter-evidence submission, and growth proposals. These are **proposed logical interfaces**, not a claim of a finalized protocol or an existing conformant implementation.

Portable Intelligence Runtime Pieces (PIRP) and Units (PIRU) are candidate carriers of informal and formalized reusable structures. Adapters should allow existing tools and services to participate without being rewritten.

**Design principle:** *Standardize the contract, not the intelligence.*

## 7. Validation: Multi-Intelligence Adversarial Sandboxes

Structural growth requires more than generating plausible new ideas. We propose a **Multi-Intelligence Adversarial Sandbox (MIAS)** with differentiated roles:

- **Builder:** proposes a structure and its performance claims.
- **Challenger:** searches for counterexamples and boundary failures.
- **Independent evaluator:** applies explicit tests and adjudicates evidence.
- **Simulation/world-model engine:** supplies controlled scenarios, perturbations, and possible trajectories.

These roles need not all be LLMs. Constraint solvers, theorem provers, simulators, statistical tests, and human experts may provide stronger independent checks for specific claims. Diversity matters because similar models can share blind spots.

Tests should include relevant baselines, ablations, distribution shifts, cost accounting, and negative results. Evaluators themselves should be subject to challenge, with bounded review depth and escalation rules.

Exploratory freedom must be separated from production authority: isolated experiments, shadow evaluation, and authorized deployment require different permissions. A sandbox is not automatically safe if it can access sensitive data or affect external systems.

## 8. From Structural Scaling to New Perception Horizons

SIF proposes a long-term research direction organized around **larger structures, deeper structural hierarchies, more precise guidance, and greater computational investment**. Complementary axes include interoperability, adversarial validation, and evidence-governed evolution.

The principal hypothesis is that validated composition may yield capabilities unavailable to any single component. Such gains are not guaranteed: coordination overhead, error propagation, and weak evaluation may erase the benefits.

We distinguish:

- **World model:** a computational representation of states, relations, dynamics, and consequences.
- **Worldview:** the higher-level organization of which entities, scales, causal explanations, and perspectives matter.
- **Structural perception horizon:** the current boundary of distinctions, relations, dynamics, and possibilities that a system can represent, detect, and evaluate.

As intelligence structures are composed, refined, and folded into reusable **macro-intelligence pieces**, a system may acquire new problem representations and discover questions previously outside its horizon. A former multi-node subsystem may become a node in a larger assembly.

This is a **research hypothesis about structural perception and worldview growth**, not a demonstrated universal law of emergence.

## 9. Perspective-Relative ASI and Open-Ended Futures

An intelligence system may exceed human performance across some tasks, domains, or evaluation perspectives without thereby exhausting all possible forms of intelligence. SIF therefore treats claims of superintelligence as requiring explicit scope and criteria.

Two propositions guide this research:

1. **Perspective-relative capability:** performance superiority depends on task distributions, resources, and evaluation perspectives.
2. **Open-ended architectural possibility:** no finite current architecture should be presumed to cover all future structures, problems, or perspectives.

Neither proposition proves that progress must continue indefinitely or that every new structure will improve performance. They motivate openness, revision, and disciplined empirical testing.

## 10. Research Questions and Falsifiable Hypotheses

The SIF agenda should be judged against measurable alternatives:

**H1 — Composition benefit.** For selected heterogeneous tasks and equal resource budgets, an SIF-style composition can outperform a strong single-model or fixed-pipeline baseline on predefined quality measures.

**H2 — Localization benefit.** Structural localization and typed relay can reduce search or execution cost while maintaining or improving task accuracy, compared with appropriate retrieval and routing baselines.

**H3 — Precedent benefit.** A registry containing applicability conditions and failure precedents can improve adaptation accuracy over a success-only capability catalog.

**H4 — Adversarial validation benefit.** Heterogeneous challengers can uncover more distinct, consequential structural failures per unit of evaluation budget than homogeneous self-critique alone.

**H5 — Perception-horizon expansion.** Certain validated macro-structural compositions can detect or formulate meaningful distinctions absent from predefined component-only baselines, under independent human or formal assessment.

Each hypothesis may fail. Negative results should be retained with task distributions, evaluation protocols, budgets, and reproducibility notes.

## 11. Proposed Research and Engineering Roadmap

**Phase A — Architecture and vocabulary:** stabilize the four domains, dual maps, structural precedents, and candidate contracts.

**Phase B — Minimal interoperability:** test naming, typing, relay, and adapters across a deliberately small heterogeneous capability set.

**Phase C — Evidence infrastructure:** define benchmark tasks, failure registries, evaluator independence criteria, and sandbox isolation.

**Phase D — Growth experiments:** compare reuse, adaptation, composition, and candidate invention against explicit baselines.

**Phase E — Higher-order intelligence:** investigate macro-intelligence pieces, cross-scale relay, perception-horizon measures, and worldview shifts.

The order is methodological, not a claim that all phases must be implemented centrally or sequentially.

## 12. Design Principles

1. **Open-ended, not all-enclosing:** future intelligence structures remain admissible.
2. **Precedent-first, not precedent-only:** reuse before invention, without suppressing discovery.
3. **Multi-perspective organization:** structures may play multiple roles.
4. **Contracts before runtime integration:** specify boundaries before relying on interoperability.
5. **Evaluation is not authorization:** policy governs action rights independently of scores.
6. **Counter-evidence before promotion:** growth claims must survive appropriate challenges.
7. **Versioned and reversible evolution:** new structures can be restricted, rolled back, or retired.
8. **Proportional complexity:** add structural depth only where measured benefits justify its costs.

## 13. Conclusion

The Structural Intelligence Framework reframes intelligence engineering from the pursuit of one all-encompassing architecture to the **organization and evolution of an open ecosystem of intelligence structures**. Its four functional domains describe where problems are localized, how specialized capabilities operate, how decisions are evaluated and governed, and how unresolved differences motivate new structural candidates. Dual maps, structural precedents, universal contracts, and relay mechanisms make the framework actionable in principle; adversarial sandboxes and counter-evidence provide a path toward disciplined validation.

SIF's most ambitious hypothesis is not merely that larger assemblies will solve more tasks. It is that evidence-governed structural growth may expand the range of phenomena and problems that an intelligence system can recognize and formulate. Establishing whether, when, and at what cost this occurs is a research program rather than an accomplished result.

> **SIF should organize intelligence without enclosing its future.**
